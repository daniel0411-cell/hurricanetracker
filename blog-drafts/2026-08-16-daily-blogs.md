# Hurricane Generator Safety: How to Avoid Carbon Monoxide Poisoning

> Meta description: Portable generators save lives during hurricane outages but are the leading cause of post-storm poisonings. Learn safe placement, CO alarms, symptoms, and fuel handling.

## Direct Answer: The One Rule That Prevents Generator Deaths

Place the generator outdoors, far from doors, windows, and vents — never inside a home, garage, basement, or shed, even with the door open. Carbon monoxide (CO) is an odorless, invisible gas that builds up within minutes and can be fatal. After hurricanes, outages lead to preventable CO poisonings from generators run too close to living spaces. The single most important action is distance: keep the unit at least 20 feet from the house with the exhaust pointing away. Everything else in this guide supports that one rule.

## Why Generators Are Dangerous After Hurricanes

Power outages after a hurricane can last days or weeks, and portable generators become the default backup for refrigerators, medical devices, and phones. That usefulness is exactly why they are risky: in the stress of an outage, people move a running generator into a garage "just for a minute" or place it under a window for convenience. CO then seeps inside. Generator-related CO incidents rise after major storms, which is why emergency managers repeat the same warning every season. Treat the generator as a hazard that demands a fixed, outdoor setup.

## The 20-Foot Rule: Where to Place Your Generator

Set the generator at least 20 feet from the home, with the engine exhaust pointed away from any opening. Place it on a dry, stable surface to avoid fuel spills and electrical shock. Never run it on a balcony, under a carport attached to the house, or beside a window where fumes can enter. If neighbors are close, angle it so exhaust does not drift toward their windows either. Before the season, choose and mark the exact outdoor spot so you are not deciding placement in the dark during a storm.

## Carbon Monoxide Alarms: Non-Negotiable

Install battery-operated or battery-backup CO alarms on every level of the home, especially near bedrooms. Test them monthly and replace batteries at the start of hurricane season. A CO alarm is your early-warning system when a generator is running nearby — without it, you may not notice the gas until symptoms appear. If the alarm sounds, treat it as an emergency: move everyone outside immediately and call 911. Do not assume it is a false trigger during an outage when a generator is in use.

## Recognizing CO Poisoning Symptoms

CO poisoning starts with headache, dizziness, weakness, nausea, and confusion — signs easily mistaken for storm stress or fatigue. As exposure continues, it causes vomiting, loss of coordination, and unconsciousness. Because the gas is colorless and odorless, people often do not realize they are being poisoned until it is severe. If multiple people in the home feel sick at the same time, suspect CO immediately. Move to fresh air and seek emergency medical help; if you have a preexisting condition, consult your doctor about your personal risk plan.

## Build a Generator Safety Go-Kit

Prepare a dedicated kit so safe operation is possible the moment the power fails.

- Heavy-duty outdoor extension cords rated for the load you will draw.
- Battery CO alarms (one per level) with fresh batteries.
- A dry, covered mat or platform to keep the generator off wet ground.
- A fire extinguisher rated for fuel fires, kept nearby but not on the generator.
- A written card with the 20-foot rule and emergency numbers.
- Weatherproof gloves and a flashlight for safe nighttime checks.

Keep this kit with your emergency supplies so it is never buried in storage during an outage.

## Fuel Storage and Refueling Safely

Store gasoline in approved containers, away from living areas and ignition sources, in a well-ventilated outdoor or detached space. Never refuel a hot or running generator — shut it off and let it cool first to avoid ignition. Spilled fuel on a hot engine can flash into flames. Rotate stored fuel with a stabilizer so it remains usable across the season. Handle fuel outdoors only, and keep children and pets away from both the generator and the cans.

## Connecting Power Without Backfeeding

Do not plug a generator into a wall outlet to "backfeed" the house. Backfeeding can energize utility lines and endanger utility workers, and it bypasses safety devices that protect your home. The safe methods are a heavy-duty extension cord to individual appliances, or a transfer switch installed by a licensed electrician before the storm. If you want whole-house backup, plan the transfer switch in calm weather — it is not a during-the-storm fix. Consult a qualified electrician about the right setup for your panel.

## Room-by-Room Power Priority

Decide in advance which devices matter most, because a portable generator has limited capacity.

- Kitchen: refrigerator and a phone charger rank first to preserve food and communication.
- Bedroom: a CPAP or medical device if prescribed — confirm the generator can handle the startup load.
- Bathroom: none critical; avoid high-draw heaters.
- Living room: a fan or radio for comfort, only if capacity remains.

Add up wattages before plugging in; overloading trips the breaker or damages equipment. If a household member depends on electric medical equipment, consult their supplier or doctor about a prescribed backup plan rather than relying on a portable generator alone.

## Special Considerations: Medical Devices and Vulnerable Members

Households with oxygen, dialysis, or powered mobility devices face extra risk during outages. A portable generator may not safely or reliably power sensitive medical equipment, and CO exposure is especially dangerous for anyone with breathing problems. Contact your medical equipment provider and doctor before storm season to plan a prescribed backup, and identify a shelter or facility along your evacuation route that can support the need. Do not assume a home generator solves a medical power requirement — confirm it with your clinician and supplier.

## What to Do If You Suspect CO Exposure

If a CO alarm sounds, or if people show symptoms, act without delay: move everyone outdoors to fresh air immediately, then call 911. Do not re-enter to shut off the generator until professionals say it is safe. Seek medical evaluation even if symptoms seem to fade, because CO effects can return. Keep the generator off and let the house air out fully before going back inside. These steps are standard emergency guidance — for personal medical concerns, follow your doctor's advice.

## Common Generator Myths

- "The garage door is open, so it's fine." CO spreads through the home regardless; garages are not safe.
- "A few feet from the window is enough." Twenty feet is the minimum; closer lets fumes enter.
- "I'll feel the gas if it's dangerous." CO has no smell; you will not notice it in time.
- "Backfeeding is a shortcut." It endangers line workers and bypasses home safety devices.

## FAQ

**Q: How far from the house should a generator be?**
**A:** At least 20 feet, with the exhaust pointing away from doors, windows, and vents. Closer placement lets carbon monoxide enter the home even if no one smells anything.

**Q: Can I run a generator in the garage with the door open?**
**A:** No. An open garage door does not prevent CO from spreading into living spaces. Always run it outdoors on a dry surface, well away from the house.

**Q: What are the symptoms of carbon monoxide poisoning?**
**A:** Headache, dizziness, nausea, weakness, and confusion that often affect several people at once. If you suspect CO, move to fresh air and call 911 immediately.

**Q: Do I need a carbon monoxide alarm if the generator is outside?**
**A:** Yes. A battery CO alarm on every level is the early-warning layer that catches drift from a neighbor's unit or an unexpected wind shift. Do not skip it.

**Q: Is it safe to refuel a running generator?**
**A:** No. Turn it off and let it cool first. Spilled fuel on a hot engine can ignite. Store fuel in approved containers away from living areas.

# Hurricane Additional Living Expenses (ALE) Coverage: What Your Policy Pays After Displacement

> Meta description: If a hurricane makes your home uninhabitable, ALE (loss-of-use) coverage can pay for hotel, food, and travel. Learn what is reimbursable, the limits, and how to document claims.

## Direct Answer: What ALE Covers

If a hurricane makes your home uninhabitable, Additional Living Expenses (ALE) coverage — also called loss-of-use — can pay the reasonable, extra costs of living elsewhere: hotel or rental, meals above your normal food spend, pet boarding, storage, and mileage for required travel. It is not a blank check; it covers the difference between your normal living costs and the extra costs forced by the displacement. Exact limits and covered items vary by policy and state, so confirm the details with your insurance company and read your policy terms before you rely on it.

## What Counts as Additional Living Expenses

ALE typically reimburses costs you would not have if you were home.

- Lodging: hotel, motel, or a temporary rental while the home is unusable.
- Meals: the extra cost of eating out versus your usual grocery spending.
- Pet boarding if shelters or rentals will not accept animals.
- Storage for salvaged belongings.
- Mileage and transit for commuting from the temporary location to work or school.
- Laundry and other incidental costs caused by the displacement.

Keep it reasonable — insurers compare your claim to your normal baseline, and "extra" is the key test.

## What ALE Usually Does Not Cover

ALE pays the added cost of displacement, not the damage itself, which is a separate structural and contents claim. It generally will not cover the mortgage or rent you still owe on the damaged home, ordinary groceries at your normal level, or luxury upgrades. It also does not replace lost income. Because every policy differs, ask your insurer which line items your specific plan includes and whether there is a separate dollar limit or a time limit on benefits.

## Coverage Limits and How They Work

ALE is often set as a percentage of your dwelling coverage (commonly 10% to 30%) or as a defined dollar amount, and some policies cap the number of months. Once the limit is reached, you pay the rest. Understanding your cap before a storm lets you budget a displacement realistically. Request a written summary of your ALE limit and any sub-limits from your agent, and store it with your policy. This is general information; your policy terms control what actually applies.

## Build a Claim-Ready Document Folder

Organize proof before you need it so the ALE claim moves fast.

- Your policy declarations page showing the ALE limit.
- A written inventory of why the home is uninhabitable (photos, adjuster notes).
- Every receipt: lodging, meals, fuel, pet boarding, storage, laundry.
- A simple log linking each expense to the displacement dates.
- Communication with your insurer, including claim numbers and names.

Keep digital copies in cloud storage and a waterproof paper folder you can grab on the way out.

## Before the Storm: Confirm Your Coverage

Do not wait for landfall to learn your ALE terms. Call your insurance company or agent and ask for your exact limit, any time cap, whether it is a separate sub-limit, and what documentation they require. If you rent, ask the same of your renters insurer, because loss-of-use is often included but with its own limits. Note the answers in your document folder. This preparation turns a confusing post-storm conversation into a routine claim.

## During Displacement: Track Every Dollar

From the first night away, save every receipt and note the date and purpose. Photograph paper receipts immediately in case they fade or are lost. Use a single credit card or a notes app to avoid scattered proof. Insurers expect itemized, dated evidence, and gaps invite disputes. The more cleanly you show the "extra" cost, the smoother the reimbursement.

## How to File an ALE Claim

Notify your insurer as soon as displacement begins, even before full damage assessment. Provide your claim number, the date you left, and why the home is unusable. Submit receipts in batches rather than one overwhelming pile, and keep a copy of everything you send. Ask for the expected timeline in writing. If your claim is delayed or reduced, request a written explanation and, if needed, contact your state insurance department. These steps are general guidance; follow your insurer's stated process.

## Room-by-Room: Proving Habitability Loss

Adjusters assess which functions the home lost.

- Kitchen: no functioning stove, fridge, or safe water means meals must be eaten out.
- Bathroom: lost plumbing or power for sanitation makes the home unsafe to occupy.
- Bedrooms: no climate control, structural damage, or contamination blocks safe sleep.
- Utilities: no power, water, or sewer for an extended period supports an ALE claim.

Photograph each failed system with dates; the clearer the habitability gap, the stronger the ALE case.

## Renters and ALE: What Applies

Renters insurance commonly includes loss-of-use coverage, but the limit is usually modest. If a hurricane makes your rental uninhabitable, you may claim hotel and meal differences just as owners do, and you generally still owe rent until the lease ends or the unit is released. Confirm your renter ALE limit with your insurer before the season, because a small cap can be exhausted quickly during a regional displacement when hotels are scarce.

## Common ALE Mistakes That Reduce Payouts

- Throwing away receipts instead of scanning them.
- Mixing normal spending with displacement spending in one pile.
- Assuming ALE is unlimited or lasts indefinitely.
- Not telling the insurer promptly that you have been displaced.
- Treating ALE as the damage claim rather than the living-expense claim.

## FAQ

**Q: Does hurricane ALE cover hotel costs?**
**A:** Often yes, when the home is uninhabitable and the hotel is a reasonable extra cost. Confirm the limit and documentation rules with your insurance company, because policies differ.

**Q: How long does ALE coverage last?**
**A:** It depends on your policy — many set a dollar limit or a time cap. Ask your insurer for the exact duration and any sub-limits before you rely on it.

**Q: Is ALE included in every homeowner policy?**
**A:** Most standard homeowner policies include it, but the amount varies. Review your declarations page or call your agent; do not assume a specific limit applies.

**Q: Does renters insurance include loss-of-use coverage?**
**A:** Usually, but with a typically smaller limit than homeowners. Confirm your renter ALE cap with your insurer before hurricane season.

**Q: What receipts do I need for an ALE claim?**
**A:** Lodging, meals above normal spending, pet boarding, storage, and travel tied to the displacement, each dated and itemized. Keep digital and paper copies.

# How to Use a Live Hurricane Tracker: Read the Cone, Alerts, and Surge Risk for Your Location

> Meta description: A practical guide to using a live hurricane tracker: set your location, read the cone of uncertainty, interpret alerts, and check storm surge and evacuation context before a storm.

## Direct Answer: What a Live Tracker Tells You

A live hurricane tracker turns official forecast data into a view you can act on: the storm's projected path, the cone of uncertainty, current alerts for your county, storm surge and flood risk, and evacuation context. It does not replace official sources — it helps you interpret them for your exact location. The right habit is to set your location first, then check the tracker daily as a storm approaches, and always confirm decisions against nhc.noaa.gov, weather.gov, and your local emergency management. This guide is educational; never treat a map as a substitute for an official advisory or evacuation order.

## Set Your Location First

Before reading anything else, enter your home, work, and any family addresses into the tracker. A hurricane's threat is local: two neighborhoods a few miles apart can face very different surge or wind. With location set, the tracker filters alerts and risk layers to what actually affects you. Do this in calm weather so you are not fumbling during a warning. If you care for others — children, older adults, neighbors — add their locations too, because your plan may need to cover more than one address.

## Reading the Cone of Uncertainty

The cone shows the probable track of the storm's center, based on the historical error of past forecasts. Crucially, the storm's effects — wind, rain, surge — often reach far outside the cone, so do not read the line as a boundary of safety. A narrower cone means higher confidence, not lower risk. The cone is a forecast, not a guarantee, and it shifts as new data arrives. For a deeper explainer, see {{INTERNAL: /blog/how-to-read-the-hurricane-cone-of-uncertainty/}}, and always cross-check the latest cone at https://www.nhc.noaa.gov/.

## Understanding the Alerts Feed

Live trackers pull active watches and warnings from official feeds. A hurricane watch means conditions are possible within 48 hours; a warning means they are expected within 36 hours and you should act. Color and wording differ by hazard — hurricane, storm surge, flood, tornado — so read the label, not just the color. When your county appears in a warning, that is your signal to execute the plan, not to keep watching. Confirm the exact wording at https://www.weather.gov/ for your area.

## Storm Surge and Flood Context

Storm surge is often the deadliest hurricane hazard, and it is mapped separately from wind. A good tracker shows surge zones and flood risk for your location, which can matter more than the category. Even areas outside the cone can flood from rain or surge. Check whether your address falls in a surge zone and pair it with your evacuation zone from {{INTERNAL: /blog/how-to-check-your-hurricane-evacuation-zone/}}. Treat any surge risk as a reason to leave early when ordered.

## Wind, Rain, and Tornado Risk Layers

Beyond the cone, trackers layer wind field size, rainfall forecasts, and tornado risk. A large storm can bring tropical-storm winds far from the center, and rain can cause inland flooding days after landfall. Tornadoes can spin up in the storm's bands, often with little lead time. Use these layers to gauge how long your preparation window is, but remember they are forecasts that change. Official severe-weather statements at https://www.weather.gov/ carry the actionable warnings.

## Evacuation Zone and Route Overlays

Many trackers link your location to a designated evacuation zone and suggested routes. This is where the map becomes a plan: if your zone is called, you already know the road you will take. Confirm the route against your county's official plan, because trackers summarize but do not replace local orders. Pair the overlay with the {{INTERNAL: /tools/preparedness-checklist/}} so the decision to leave is backed by a packed kit. Leave when officials say, not when the screen looks calm.

## Build a Tracking Routine (Timeline)

A tracker is most useful as a habit, not a panic tool.

- Early season: set locations and learn the interface.
- When a storm forms: check once daily.
- At hurricane watch: check morning and evening.
- At warning: check per official update, and act on alerts, not just the map.

Routine reduces the chance you miss a shift. The goal is to decide early, while options remain.

## Share Updates With Your Household

A tracker only helps if everyone sees the same picture. Share your location setup and the latest reading with family through your {{INTERNAL: /blog/hurricane-family-communication-plan/}}, so no one acts on an old or different source. Designate one person to post the official call — watch, warning, evacuate — to the group, and agree in advance to trust that message over social media rumors. Clear sharing prevents the confusion that causes late, dangerous departures.

## Common Tracker Misreading Mistakes

- Treating the cone's edge as a safe line — impacts extend outside it.
- Watching only the center and ignoring surge and flood layers.
- Trusting a single snapshot instead of the trend across updates.
- Letting the map replace official evacuation orders.
- Checking only once, then assuming nothing changed.

## When to Stop Relying on the Screen and Act

When your county is under a warning or an evacuation order, the time for analysis is over — execute the plan. Trackers help you prepare; they do not grant permission to wait. If you are in a surge or evacuation zone, leave at the order, not at the last forecast. Power and data may fail as the storm arrives, so decide while you still have full information. Official orders at https://www.weather.gov/ and from local emergency management are the final word.

## Tracking After Landfall: Inland Risks

A tracker remains useful after a hurricane makes landfall, when the threat shifts to inland flooding, tornadoes, and stalled rain. Keep checking official feeds for flood warnings and river gauges even if the storm "weakened." The {{INTERNAL: /hurricane-tracker/live/}} view and local alerts together show where the danger moved. Do not assume safety just because the wind dropped — post-landfall flooding causes many hurricane deaths.

## FAQ

**Q: How accurate is the hurricane cone?**
**A:** The cone reflects the historical average error of the storm center's track; it is a probability, not a guarantee, and the storm's effects often reach outside it. Always check the latest official cone at nhc.noaa.gov.

**Q: Should I trust a tracker more than official alerts?**
**A:** No. A tracker helps you interpret official data for your location, but official watches, warnings, and evacuation orders are the final authority. Use the map to prepare, not to override them.

**Q: What does the cone not show?**
**A:** It shows the likely center track, not the full wind field, storm surge, rainfall flooding, or tornado risk — all of which can occur far from the line. Check those separate layers.

**Q: How often is live tracker data updated?**
**A:** Reputable trackers refresh as official forecasts and alerts are issued, typically multiple times per day during an active storm. Still confirm against weather.gov and nhc.noaa.gov for the newest advisory.

**Q: Can I track a hurricane on my phone?**
**A:** Yes. Most live trackers are mobile-friendly, and setting your location on a phone lets you receive alerts on the go. Keep a battery backup, because power and data may fail near landfall.
