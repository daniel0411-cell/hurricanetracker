# Hurricane Boat & Marina Preparedness: Haul-Out, Securing, and Storm Plans

## Direct Answer
Boats are among the most damage-prone possessions in a hurricane. The safest option is to haul your vessel out of the water and store it on high ground well before the storm; if that is not possible, double up dock lines, remove loose gear, and close seacocks. Never stay aboard during a hurricane — follow the same evacuation orders as the rest of your household. Confirm your marina's written hurricane plan and your insurance coverage before season starts. Track the approaching threat at {{INTERNAL: /hurricane-tracker/live/}}.

## Why Boats Are High-Risk in Hurricanes
Storm surge, breaking waves, and flying debris destroy docks and batter hulls. Even a "secure" tied vessel can be crushed by neighboring boats or lifted onto land. Marinas in V and A flood zones face the worst surge; know your zone via {{INTERNAL: /blog/hurricane-flood-zone-map-fema-understanding/}}.

## Know Your Marina's Hurricane Plan
Most storm-ready marinas publish a hurricane plan covering haul-out priority, deadlines, and liability. Read it before the season, note the cutoff time, and ask whether haul-out is first-come-first-served. Some require you to remove your own boat or use a named contractor.

## The Haul-Out Decision: When and How
Decide early — travel lifts and trailers book up fast. Haul out when the National Hurricane Center issues a watch for your area or when your marina sets its deadline, whichever comes first. Store the boat on approved high ground, not in a low-lying lot that floods. Block the keel, support the hull, and deflate tires slightly for stability.

## If You Can't Haul Out: Securing at the Dock
- Use long spring lines so the boat can rise with surge.
- Add fenders and chafing gear at every contact point.
- Remove canvas, electronics, outboards, and loose items.
- Close seacocks and disconnect shore power.
- Do not rely on the boat as a place to shelter.

## Pre-Storm Boat Checklist
- Registration and documentation in a waterproof bag (see {{INTERNAL: /blog/hurricane-documents-insurance-checklist/}}).
- Spare lines, fenders, and shaft keys ashore.
- Electronics and personal items removed.
- Fuel topped and tank vents sealed.
- A written plan shared with your household ({{INTERNAL: /blog/hurricane-family-communication-plan/}}).

## Documentation and Insurance Before the Storm
Photograph the hull, serial numbers, and electronics now. Confirm whether your policy covers named-storm damage, the deductible, and the hauling deadline. Wind and flood are often treated differently; review your policy or contact your insurer for specifics. File claims promptly through {{INTERNAL: /blog/hurricane-insurance-claim-guide-documentation/}}.

## Fuel, Batteries, and Systems Prep
Top off and stabilize fuel to limit condensation. Disconnect batteries unless the marina requires shore power off. Tape or plug through-hull openings. Label everything you remove so reassembly is faster after the storm.

## Safety: Never Stay With the Boat
No possession is worth a life. Once local officials issue an evacuation order for your zone, leave with your household. Coordinate through your evacuation checklist at {{INTERNAL: /blog/hurricane-evacuation-checklist/}}.

## After the Storm: Damage Assessment
Do not board a damaged vessel until the marina confirms docks are safe. Photograph damage for insurance before any cleanup. Check for fuel spills and report them to the marina and local authorities. Beware post-storm contractor fraud ({{INTERNAL: /blog/hurricane-contractor-scams-insurance-claim/}}).

## Working With Your Marina and Neighbors
Share your plan, offer to help less-prepared neighbors haul out, and agree on a post-storm check-in. A coordinated dock suffers less chaos. Local emergency management guidance is at https://www.ready.gov/ and https://www.fema.gov/.

## Tracking the Threat on the Water
Watch the National Hurricane Center (https://www.nhc.noaa.gov/) for the cone and surge forecasts, and the National Weather Service (https://www.weather.gov/) for local marine warnings. Our live tracker at {{INTERNAL: /hurricane-tracker/live/}} summarizes both.

## Connect to Your Household Plan
Your boat plan is part of a bigger picture. Build the family readiness checklist with {{INTERNAL: /tools/preparedness-checklist/}} so evacuation, documents, and communication all line up.

## FAQ
**Q:** Should I ride out the hurricane on my boat?
**A:** No. Staying aboard during a hurricane is extremely dangerous. Evacuate with your household when local officials order it, following the same rules as everyone else.

**Q:** Is hauling out always better than dock lines?
**A:** In almost all cases, yes — a hauled, properly supported boat on high ground survives far better than a docked one. The trade-off is cost and lift availability, so decide early.

**Q:** Does boat insurance cover hurricane damage?
**A:** Coverage varies by policy, and named-storm deductibles and haul-out deadlines are common. Review your policy or contact your insurer for the exact terms that apply to you.

**Q:** What should I remove from the boat before a storm?
**A:** Take electronics, outboards, canvas, personal items, and anything loose that could become debris. Leave the hull secured with closed seacocks and disconnected shore power.

**Q:** Who decides when the marina closes?
**A:** The marina operator sets haul-out deadlines in their hurricane plan, but evacuation orders come from local officials. Follow the stricter of the two, and never stay past an official order.

# Helping Children Cope With Hurricane Anxiety: A Parent's Mental-Health Guide

## Direct Answer
Children feel safer when adults are calm, honest, and prepared. Before a storm, explain what will happen in simple words and involve kids in a small task like packing their comfort item. During the event, keep routines and limit scary news. Afterward, listen and let them help with recovery. If fear lasts more than a few weeks, wakes them nightly, or changes eating or school behavior, consult your pediatrician or a licensed mental-health professional. General guidance for families is at https://www.ready.gov/ and https://www.fema.gov/.

## Why Storms Trigger Anxiety in Kids
Children imagine worst cases they cannot verify, and they read adult stress even when we stay quiet. Loud wind, sirens, and displacement from home feel like a loss of safety. Preparation replaces helplessness with a sense of control.

## Signs of Hurricane-Related Stress by Age
- **Toddlers:** clinginess, sleep regression, thumb-sucking.
- **School-age:** stomachaches, irritability, fear of being alone.
- **Teens:** withdrawal, anger, trouble concentrating.
Adults with disabilities or prior trauma may need extra support (see {{INTERNAL: /blog/hurricane-safety-seniors-children-disability/}}).

## Talk to Children Before the Storm
Use plain language: "A big storm is coming. We have a plan and we will stay together." Answer questions truthfully but briefly. Avoid overexplaining damage or death. Reassure them the adults are in charge of safety.

## Build a Sense of Control With a Plan
Give each child one age-ready job — filling the pet's bowl, grabbing the flashlight, checking the radio. A shared family plan at {{INTERNAL: /blog/hurricane-family-communication-plan/}} shows them exactly what happens and who is responsible.

## Comfort Items and Routine During the Event
Keep a favorite toy, blanket, or book in the go-kit ({{INTERNAL: /blog/hurricane-emergency-kit-checklist/}}). Keep meal and bedtime routines as normal as possible. Quiet activities beat screen-scrolling when the power is out.

## Limit News and Adult Talk
Constant coverage and adult worrying raise child anxiety. Mute alerts around kids, and save serious conversations for private adult time. Check official updates yourself at {{INTERNAL: /hurricane-tracker/live/}} instead of leaving the TV on.

## After the Storm: Helping Kids Process
Let them help with safe tasks like sorting donations or drawing the experience. Name feelings: "It's okay to feel scared." Keep answers simple and reconfirm the home is being made safe. More recovery steps at {{INTERNAL: /blog/what-to-do-after-a-hurricane/}}.

## When to Seek Professional Help
If fear persists beyond two to four weeks, causes nightly waking, refusal to separate, or school avoidance, consult your pediatrician or a licensed counselor. Local health departments and disaster crisis lines can point you to help; assistance programs are listed at https://www.disasterassistance.gov/.

## Special Considerations for Disabilities or Trauma
Children with autism, sensory needs, or past trauma may need visual schedules, noise-reducing headphones, or familiar objects. Plan these into the kit early and coordinate with their care team — your doctor or therapist can advise on specifics.

## Involve the Whole Family Communication Plan
A clear plan reduces guessing. Use {{INTERNAL: /blog/hurricane-family-communication-plan/}} so kids know where adults will be and how the family reconnects if separated.

## Connect to Preparedness So Kids Feel Ready
Confidence comes from doing, not just talking. Walk through the household readiness checklist with {{INTERNAL: /tools/preparedness-checklist/}} so children see that the family is ready.

## Trusted Resources for Parents
Ready.gov (https://www.ready.gov/) and FEMA (https://www.fema.gov/) offer family planning tools; the National Weather Service (https://www.weather.gov/) and National Hurricane Center (https://www.nhc.noaa.gov/) provide calm, factual storm information you can share age-appropriately.

## FAQ
**Q:** How do I explain a hurricane to a young child?
**A:** Keep it simple and reassuring: name the storm, say the adults have a plan, and describe the concrete steps you will take. Avoid graphic details about damage.

**Q:** Is it normal for kids to act out after a storm?
**A:** Yes. Clinginess, sleep changes, and irritability are common short-term responses. They usually ease within weeks as routine returns; if not, consult your pediatrician.

**Q:** Should children watch hurricane news coverage?
**A:** Limit it. Repeated alarming footage raises anxiety. Check updates yourself via official sources and share only what they need to know.

**Q:** My teen shuts down and won't talk — what helps?
**A:** Don't force it. Offer presence, a small job, and private check-ins. If withdrawal lasts or worsens, a school counselor or mental-health professional can help; your doctor can advise.

**Q:** When is storm anxiety a sign to get professional help?
**A:** If fear lasts more than a few weeks, causes nightly waking, separation refusal, or school problems, consult your pediatrician or a licensed mental-health professional for guidance.

# Emergency Water Storage and Purification for Hurricanes: How Much to Store and How to Keep It Safe

## Direct Answer
Store at least one gallon of water per person per day for drinking and sanitation, and plan for a minimum of three days — ideally two weeks if space allows. Use food-grade containers, keep them sealed and cool, and rotate the supply every six months. If your tap water is later declared unsafe, boil it for one minute (or use an EPA-registered purifier) before drinking. Build this into your kit via {{INTERNAL: /blog/hurricane-emergency-kit-checklist/}}. Official storage guidance is at https://www.ready.gov/ and https://www.fema.gov/.

## How Much Water You Actually Need
Plan one gallon per person per day, plus more for hot weather, pregnancy, illness, or active cleanup. Don't forget pets (see {{INTERNAL: /blog/hurricane-pet-safety/}}) and any medical devices that use water. A two-week supply reduces reliance on uncertain post-storm distribution.

## Best Containers for Storage
Use BPA-free, food-grade jugs with tight seals. Commercial bottled water is simplest; for refills, use cleaned soda bottles or dedicated water bricks. Avoid milk jugs — they trap bacteria and go brittle. Label each container with the fill date.

## Where and How to Store Water
Keep containers in a cool, dark place away from chemicals and gasoline. A utility shelf or closet works; a cleaned bathtub lined with a dedicated aqua-safe bag is a common last-minute boost. Keep some in your go-kit in case you evacuate ({{INTERNAL: /blog/hurricane-evacuation-checklist/}}).

## Commercial vs. Homemade Storage
Store-bought water is reliably safe and dated. Homemade storage saves money but needs sanitizing: wash containers with dish soap, rinse with a diluted bleach solution (unscented), and air-dry. Never use containers that held chemicals or milk.

## Purifying Questionable Water Safely
If you must use water from an unknown source, purify it first. Boiling is the most reliable: bring to a rolling boil for one minute (longer at high elevation), then cool covered. Otherwise use an EPA-registered filter or disinfectant labeled for drinking water. When in doubt, follow local boil-water notices.

## Boiling, Filters, and Tablets Compared
- **Boiling:** kills most pathogens; needs heat and fuel.
- **Filters:** good for sediment and many organisms; check the label for viruses.
- **Tablets/drops:** portable and simple; follow the exact contact time.
No method replaces clean storage — purify only when your stored supply runs out.

## What NOT to Drink After a Storm
Do not drink from flooded wells, storm drains, or standing pools. Do not use water under a boil advisory for brushing teeth or baby formula until it is boiled or purified. If a "do not use" order is issued, use only sealed stored water or bottled.

## Water for Pets and Family Members With Needs
Pets need roughly one ounce per pound per day; store extra. Infants, pregnant people, and those with medical conditions may need more — your doctor can advise on amounts and safe preparation, especially for formula.

## Integrating Water Into Your Kit
Pair water with the food and medicine plan in {{INTERNAL: /blog/hurricane-power-outage-food-medicine-plan/}} so the whole household supply is balanced. Keep a small carry bottle in each go-bag.

## When Tap Water Advisories Are Issued
Local utilities issue "boil water" or "do not use" notices after floods or outages. Follow them exactly and share them through your household plan. Track local alerts and outages via {{INTERNAL: /hurricane-tracker/live/}} and the National Weather Service at https://www.weather.gov/.

## Tracking Outages and Boil Notices
The National Hurricane Center (https://www.nhc.noaa.gov/) shows the storm; your county utility and NWS post water notices. Our tracker at {{INTERNAL: /hurricane-tracker/live/}} aggregates official guidance in one place.

## Connect Water to Your Full Plan
Water is one pillar of readiness. Complete the household checklist with {{INTERNAL: /tools/preparedness-checklist/}} so storage, food, power, and communication all line up before the season.

## FAQ
**Q:** How much water should I store per person?
**A:** At least one gallon per person per day for drinking and sanitation, for a minimum of three days — ideally two weeks. Hot weather, pregnancy, illness, or pets increase the amount.

**Q:** Can I store tap water in empty milk jugs?
**A:** It is not recommended. Milk residues are hard to sanitize and the plastic goes brittle. Use food-grade BPA-free jugs or cleaned soda bottles instead.

**Q:** How long is stored water good for?
**A:** Commercial bottled water keeps until its printed date; homemade stored water is best rotated every six months and kept sealed, cool, and dark.

**Q:** Is boiling enough to make flood water safe?
**A:** Boiling for one minute kills most germs and is reliable for drinking, but it will not remove chemical contamination. If chemicals are suspected, use only sealed stored water and follow local official notices.

**Q:** What does a "boil water" notice mean?
**A:** It means your tap water may be contaminated and must be boiled or otherwise purified before drinking, brushing teeth, or making baby formula. Follow the exact notice from your utility or local health department.
