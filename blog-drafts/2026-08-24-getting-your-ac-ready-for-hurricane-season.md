# Getting Your AC Ready for Hurricane Season: A Safe Checklist

Getting your AC ready for hurricane season is less about last-minute equipment work and more about reducing avoidable damage, preparing for power loss, and knowing when to stop using the system. A hurricane can affect air conditioning through windborne debris, flooding, voltage changes, damaged electrical service, blocked drainage, roof damage, and long outages. The safest plan combines routine maintenance, a clear shutdown decision, safe generator boundaries, and a realistic way to manage heat if cooling is unavailable.

This guide is general preparation information, not a substitute for the equipment manual, a licensed HVAC contractor, an electrician, the utility, an insurer, or local emergency officials. Do not open electrical panels, handle refrigerant, climb onto a wet or windy roof, modify wiring, or work around floodwater. During an active threat, use the latest official information from [weather.gov](https://www.weather.gov/) and the [National Hurricane Center](https://www.nhc.noaa.gov/).

## Start Before a Storm Is Forecast

The best time to prepare an air conditioner is during normal seasonal maintenance, not after a hurricane watch creates a rush for service. Schedule inspection early enough to repair known problems, replace a failing thermostat battery, clean accessible areas, and understand how the system should be shut down and restarted.

A pre-season appointment can also clarify which equipment belongs to you and which belongs to a landlord, condominium association, or building manager. Ask the technician to identify the indoor air handler, outdoor condenser, disconnect, thermostat, drain, filters, and any surge-protection equipment. Do not assume every system has the same controls or restart procedure.

## Schedule Routine HVAC Maintenance

Routine maintenance cannot make an air conditioner hurricane-proof, but it can uncover ordinary faults before the system is stressed by heat or unstable power. A qualified technician may inspect electrical connections, refrigerant performance, drainage, mounting, controls, filters, and overall condition according to the equipment and local requirements.

Ask for written findings and prioritize safety-related repairs. If the contractor recommends replacement, anchoring, electrical changes, refrigerant work, or structural modifications, request a clear scope and verify licensing, permits, manufacturer requirements, and warranty implications. Avoid emergency sales pressure and promises that any single accessory will protect against every hurricane hazard.

## Inspect the Outdoor Area From the Ground

Look around the outdoor condenser without removing covers or reaching into the cabinet. Clear ordinary loose objects that could become debris, such as lightweight furniture, planters, toys, tools, branches, or unsecured decorations. Keep the equipment's required airflow clear according to the manufacturer rather than packing objects tightly around it.

Check whether the unit appears level, firmly mounted, corroded, physically damaged, or exposed to runoff. Do not attempt to lift, strap, drill, or relocate it yourself. Fastening methods must account for the equipment, pad, structure, wind requirements, flood exposure, vibration, drainage, and local code. A licensed contractor should evaluate any questionable installation.

## Do Not Cover the Unit During Operation

An outdoor condenser needs airflow while operating. Improvised tarps, plastic, plywood, or tightly fitted covers can block airflow, damage the system, trap heat, or become windborne debris. Never run the air conditioner with a storm cover installed unless the manufacturer explicitly designed and approved that setup.

If a manufacturer-approved protective cover is appropriate for a fully shut-down system, follow the product and equipment instructions exactly and remove it before restart. Do not install a cover when winds, lightning, flooding, or evacuation conditions make outdoor work unsafe.

## Review Wind and Flood Exposure Separately

Wind and water create different problems. Wind can move debris or damage connections, while storm surge, river flooding, or rainfall can submerge electrical and mechanical components. A unit that is well anchored for wind may still be vulnerable to water, and elevation work that ignores structure or access can create new hazards.

Use official local flood and evacuation information rather than relying only on hurricane category or distance from the coast. The storm-surge guide at {{INTERNAL: /hurricane-tracker/storm-surge-map/}} explains why coastal water risk differs from wind category. Any elevation, platform, roof, anchoring, or flood-protection project should be designed and permitted by qualified professionals.

## Know How Your System Is Safely Shut Down

Ask the HVAC contractor how your specific equipment should be shut down before a storm and what conditions require utility or electrical guidance. A thermostat setting alone may not isolate equipment from electrical hazards. Conversely, operating breakers or disconnects without understanding the system can be dangerous.

Do not wait until wind or floodwater reaches the property to learn the procedure. Write the instructions in the household plan and make sure responsible adults can identify the correct controls. If local officials, the utility, manufacturer instructions, or a qualified professional direct shutdown, follow that guidance.

## Prepare for Power Surges and Voltage Problems

Hurricanes can produce outages, repeated power interruptions, and voltage conditions that may damage equipment. Ask a licensed electrician or qualified HVAC professional whether the home and system have appropriate, code-compliant surge protection and whether any existing device needs inspection or replacement.

Plug-in strips are not a substitute for properly designed protection for permanently wired HVAC equipment. Do not add devices inside an electrical panel or disconnect unless you are qualified and authorized to do so. Even good surge protection has limits and cannot guarantee that equipment survives a direct strike, flood, utility failure, or severe wiring damage.

## Change Filters and Check Accessible Drainage

A clean, correctly sized filter can help normal airflow and indoor air quality before an outage, but use only the type specified for the system. Installing a filter that is too restrictive may reduce performance. Replace or clean it according to the manufacturer and contractor guidance.

Check only drainage points that are safely accessible and intended for homeowner maintenance. A blocked condensate drain can contribute to water damage, but chemicals, compressed air, disassembly, or work in attics and tight spaces can create hazards. Call a professional if drainage is slow, leaking, inaccessible, or repeatedly blocked.

## Plan for Heat Without Air Conditioning

An outage can make indoor heat dangerous, especially for infants, older adults, pregnant people, people with chronic illness, and anyone using temperature-sensitive medication or medical equipment. Ask a doctor or pharmacist about individual health and medication needs before hurricane season. Do not assume a fan is enough during extreme heat, especially when indoor temperatures continue to rise.

Choose a backup location with reliable cooling outside the threatened area, identify transportation, and keep contact information available on paper. Prepare water according to official guidance, lightweight clothing, shaded areas, battery-powered communication, and a plan to leave if the home becomes unsafe. Follow local heat and emergency instructions.

## Keep Generators Separate From HVAC Guesswork

A portable generator must operate outdoors and away from doors, windows, vents, garages, crawlspaces, and other openings because carbon monoxide can kill without warning. Follow the manufacturer, [FEMA](https://www.fema.gov/), [Ready.gov](https://www.ready.gov/), fire officials, and local requirements.

Never connect a portable generator directly to household wiring, an outlet, or HVAC equipment through an improvised cord. Running central air often requires substantial power and properly designed transfer equipment. Only qualified electricians and HVAC professionals should determine whether a generator, transfer switch, load-management system, or permanently installed backup system can safely support the equipment.

## Prepare Apartments, Condos, and Rental Homes

Renters should not modify, anchor, cover, disconnect, or access shared HVAC equipment without authorization. Ask the property manager who controls the system, how outage and restart decisions are communicated, whether elevators or water pumps depend on power, and what cooling options exist if the building becomes unsafe.

Condo owners should clarify responsibility for rooftop units, balcony equipment, common electrical systems, generators, drainage, and exterior modifications. Association rules do not replace permits, manufacturer requirements, or emergency orders. Keep building contacts, insurance information, and written procedures with the evacuation plan.

## Photograph Equipment and Save Records

Take clear ground-level photos of the outdoor unit, indoor equipment where safely accessible, model and serial labels, thermostat, surrounding area, and any pre-existing damage. Save maintenance invoices, warranties, equipment manuals, contractor contacts, and insurance information in secure digital storage and a portable document set.

Review coverage, deductibles, exclusions, flood questions, and claim procedures with your insurer and policy. Insurance terms vary, and this guide cannot determine whether a particular loss is covered. After damage, document conditions before non-emergency cleanup when safe and follow the insurer's instructions.

## What To Do When a Hurricane Is Approaching

When an official threat develops, finish ordinary preparation before conditions deteriorate. Bring loose yard objects inside, confirm the safe shutdown plan, charge devices, cool the home while reliable power remains if appropriate, and prepare to follow evacuation or shelter instructions. Do not perform outdoor equipment work during lightning, rising wind, flooding, darkness, or an evacuation order.

Check current official storm information through {{INTERNAL: /hurricane-tracker/live/}} and local alerts through {{INTERNAL: /alerts/}}. The forecast cone does not show every wind, flood, surge, tornado, or outage hazard, so local instructions control local action.

## After the Storm: Inspect Before Restarting

Do not restart air conditioning just because utility power returns. First wait for official reentry, avoid floodwater and downed lines, and inspect from a safe distance. If equipment was submerged, struck, shifted, opened, exposed to debris, connected to damaged wiring, or affected by fire or structural damage, leave it off and contact qualified professionals.

Repeatedly cycling breakers or attempting to dry electrical equipment can cause shock, fire, or further damage. A licensed electrician, HVAC contractor, utility, building official, or manufacturer may need to assess the system. Document damage and contact the insurer before non-emergency repair or disposal when safe.

## AC Hurricane-Season Checklist

- Schedule routine HVAC inspection before peak demand.
- Save the manual, model information, contractor, and warranty records.
- Clear loose outdoor debris without blocking required airflow.
- Ask a professional about mounting, corrosion, flood exposure, and surge protection.
- Learn the equipment-specific shutdown and restart procedure.
- Replace the filter with the specified type.
- Check only safely accessible homeowner-maintenance drainage points.
- Prepare a backup cooling destination and transportation.
- Plan for medical and temperature-sensitive medication needs with a doctor or pharmacist.
- Keep portable generators outdoors and never improvise HVAC wiring.
- Photograph equipment before storm season.
- Wait for safe reentry and professional assessment after flood or equipment damage.

## FAQ

**Q: Should I turn off my AC before a hurricane?**

**A:** Follow the equipment manual and current guidance from your utility, local officials, HVAC contractor, or electrician. Learn the correct procedure before a storm; do not operate unfamiliar breakers or disconnects during dangerous conditions.

**Q: Should I cover my outside AC unit during a hurricane?**

**A:** Do not cover an operating unit or use an improvised cover. If the manufacturer approves a protective cover for a fully shut-down system, follow its instructions and remove it before restart. Never install it when outdoor conditions are unsafe.

**Q: Can I strap down an AC condenser myself?**

**A:** Do not improvise anchoring. Mounting must account for the equipment, pad, structure, wind, vibration, flood exposure, code, permits, and warranty. Ask a licensed HVAC contractor or other qualified professional.

**Q: Can a portable generator run central air conditioning?**

**A:** That depends on equipment loads and a properly designed electrical system. Never connect a generator through an outlet or improvised wiring. A licensed electrician and HVAC professional should determine safe capacity, transfer equipment, and installation.

**Q: Can I restart my AC after floodwater reaches it?**

**A:** Leave it off. Flooded electrical and mechanical equipment can create shock, fire, contamination, and equipment hazards. Wait for safe reentry and assessment by qualified professionals, and contact the insurer.

**Q: How can I stay cool if the power is out?**

**A:** Prepare a backup cooling location and transport before the storm, monitor indoor heat, drink safe water, and follow local heat guidance. People with medical risks or temperature-sensitive medication should consult a doctor or pharmacist in advance.

**Q: Does hurricane insurance cover AC damage?**

**A:** Coverage depends on the policy, cause of damage, deductibles, exclusions, flood coverage, and documentation. Review the policy and ask the insurer before hurricane season; do not assume wind and flood losses are treated the same way.
