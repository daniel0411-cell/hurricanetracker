# Safe Hurricane Debris Cleanup and Disposal: Hazards, Sorting, and FEMA Removal Explained

## Direct Answer
Wait until local officials say it is safe to return and begin cleanup, then protect yourself before you touch anything. Wear sturdy gloves, closed-toe boots, long sleeves, eye protection, and a respirator or mask rated for dust and mold. Sort debris into separate piles — vegetation, construction material, appliances, and household hazardous waste — because most curbside programs collect each type separately. Never run a chain saw above shoulder height, near power lines, or in flooded areas, and keep children and pets away from the work zone. Review guidance from the Federal Emergency Management Agency at https://www.fema.gov/ and your county emergency management office before you start. As the storm approaches, follow its position with {{INTERNAL: /hurricane-tracker/live/}} and act on official orders rather than on social posts.

## Why Post-Storm Debris Is More Dangerous Than the Wind
The wind gets the attention, but most hurricane injuries after landfall come from cleanup, not from the storm itself. Nails, broken glass, splintered wood, displaced furniture, and hidden sharp metal turn a normal yard into a hazard field. The National Weather Service warns that storm debris and downed trees are a leading cause of post-hurricane injuries (https://www.weather.gov/). Treat every pile as if it contains something that can cut, crush, or shock you, and move slowly. A careful, well-planned cleanup protects you far better than a fast one.

## Start With a Safety Walk-Around Before You Touch Anything
Before lifting or dragging anything, walk the property perimeter and look for dangers you cannot see from the house. Watch for downed power lines, leaning trees, sagging roofs, weakened fences, and standing water that may hide sharp objects or live wires. If you see a downed line, stay far back and report it to your utility or local emergency number — do not approach it. For related fuel and equipment hazards, the safety steps in {{INTERNAL: /blog/hurricane-generator-safety-carbon-monoxide/}} also apply near downed lines. Only begin hands-on work once you are confident the area is stable and free of immediate electrical risk.

## Personal Protective Equipment (PPE) You Should Wear
The right gear prevents most常见 cleanup injuries. At minimum, wear:
- Heavy-duty work gloves (leather or coated) to block splinters and cuts.
- Closed-toe boots with thick soles and ankle support.
- Long sleeves and long pants to protect skin from scratches and contaminants.
- Safety glasses or goggles to shield your eyes from dust and flying debris.
- An N95 or higher respirator if you are near mold, dust, or insulation.
- A hard hat when working under damaged trees or structures.
Replace wet or damaged PPE promptly, and wash exposed skin with soap and clean water when you finish for the day.

## Sharp, Heavy, and Hidden Hazards in Storm Debris
Hurricane debris hides hazards that are easy to miss. Nails can be driven up through boards, sheet metal can have razor edges, and furniture may contain broken springs or glass. Lift with your legs, not your back, and never throw debris where someone else is standing. Keep a basic first-aid kit nearby, and clean any puncture wound promptly — then check with your doctor about tetanus and wound care, because only a medical professional can advise on your specific situation. Teaching every household member to recognize these hazards is part of a solid plan in {{INTERNAL: /blog/hurricane-home-preparedness-room-by-room-checklist/}}.

## Chain Saw and Power-Equipment Safety
Chain saws cause a large share of serious cleanup injuries, so use them only if you are trained and sober. Never operate a saw above shoulder height, alone, or while standing on an unstable surface. Keep the bar away from dirt and metal, shut it off before moving, and take frequent breaks to avoid fatigue. For anything near power lines or spanning a roadway, hire a licensed tree or utility professional instead. The safety principles in {{INTERNAL: /blog/hurricane-generator-safety-carbon-monoxide/}} also apply to any gasoline-powered cleanup equipment — refuel only when the engine is cool and outdoors.

## Sorting Debris for Curbside Collection
Most municipal and county cleanup programs ask residents to separate materials into distinct piles at the curb. A typical separation looks like this:
- **Vegetation:** branches, leaves, trunks, and shrub debris.
- **Construction and demolition:** lumber, drywall, flooring, and fixtures.
- **Appliances and white goods:** refrigerators, washers, air conditioners.
- **Household hazardous waste:** paint, oil, batteries, pesticides, propane.
- **Bagged household trash:** regular garbage, separate from bulk debris.
Check your local government's website for the exact categories and placement rules, because mixing piles can delay pickup. Clear, sorted piles help crews work faster and reduce the chance that heavy equipment spreads contaminants.

## What FEMA's Debris Removal Program Covers
After a major disaster declaration, the federal government may reimburse local governments for eligible debris removal on public rights-of-way and, in some cases, private property that blocks access. The program is administered through your state and local government, not directly through individual homeowner requests in most situations. Eligibility and scope depend on the specific disaster declaration and local agreements, so confirm current details with your county emergency management office or at https://www.fema.gov/. This is separate from any insurance claim you may file, and the two processes do not replace each other.

## Household Hazardous Waste and Chemicals
Spilled fuel, paint, pesticides, and pool chemicals are common after a flood and need special handling. Never pour them down storm drains or onto the ground, and never mix unknown liquids together. Place leaking containers in a ventilated outdoor area away from ignition sources until your community's hazardous-waste collection can take them. If you are unsure whether a substance is hazardous, treat it as hazardous and ask your local waste authority how to dispose of it. The disposal steps in {{INTERNAL: /blog/what-to-do-after-a-hurricane/}} pair well with a careful hazardous-waste plan.

## Tree Limbs, Branches, and Vegetative Debris
Vegetative debris is usually the largest and easiest category to manage, but it still demands care. Cut limbs into manageable lengths only if you can do so safely, and stack them where they will not block drains or sightlines. Avoid piling branches against your home or fence, where they can hide pests or hold moisture against wood. If a tree is leaning on a structure or utility line, leave it for a licensed arborist or utility crew — do not pull it yourself. Proper handling also reduces the mold risk described in {{INTERNAL: /blog/hurricane-mold-prevention-after-flood/}}.

## Construction and Demolition Debris From Your Home
Damaged building materials — drywall, insulation, flooring, and framing — often carry dust, mold, and broken fasteners. Wear a respirator when cutting or handling them, and double-bag gypsum dust to keep it from spreading. Separate clean wood from treated or painted material where your program requires it. Keep this debris away from children and pets, and document damaged items with photos before you discard them, since your insurer may want evidence. The documentation habits in {{INTERNAL: /blog/hurricane-insurance-claim-guide-documentation/}} are worth repeating here.

## Appliances, Electronics, and White Goods
Flooded refrigerators, washers, and electronics can trap water, harbor mold, and hold a charge even when unplugged. Unplug and move them only if the area is dry and safe, and never assume a submerged appliance is dead. Many programs require doors removed from refrigerators and freezers so children and animals cannot become trapped. Check with your local collection point about whether freon-containing units need special handling. If an appliance was in contact with sewage or chemical floodwater, note that on your claim photos as well.

## Avoiding Injury From Wildlife and Contaminants
Standing water and overturned structures attract snakes, insects, and rodents displaced by the storm. Use a stick to probe debris before reaching in, and never place your hands where you cannot see. Contaminated floodwater can carry bacteria and sharp objects, so cover cuts and wash thoroughly after any exposure. If you develop redness, swelling, or fever after a wound, contact your doctor for guidance rather than treating it yourself. Keeping pets leashed and away from debris piles is part of the animal-safety approach in {{INTERNAL: /blog/hurricane-pet-safety/}}.

## When to Hire a Licensed Professional vs Do It Yourself
Some jobs are simply not safe for untrained residents. Call a licensed contractor, arborist, electrician, or utility crew for:
- Trees on power lines or across roadways.
- Structural damage, sagging roofs, or unstable walls.
- Any electrical, gas, or plumbing system affected by water.
- Large tree removal or climbing work.
Be wary of unlicensed door-to-door operators; verify credentials and written estimates first. The warning signs are covered in {{INTERNAL: /blog/hurricane-contractor-scams-insurance-claim/}}, which explains how to protect both your safety and your claim.

## Building a Debris Cleanup Plan Before the Season
The easiest cleanup is the one you prepared for in calm weather. Before hurricane season, identify where you will stage sorted piles, store PPE, and locate your community's hazardous-waste drop-off. Photograph your property in normal condition so damage is easier to document later, and keep a written list of local collection rules. A pre-staged plan also supports the broader timeline in {{INTERNAL: /blog/hurricane-preparation-timeline-72-48-24-hours/}}. General household preparedness guidance is also available from the federal readiness portal at https://www.ready.gov/. When the storm passes, you will be able to move from safety check to sorting without scrambling for gear.

## FAQ

**Q:** Who is responsible for removing debris from my private yard after a hurricane?
**A:** In most cases, homeowners are responsible for clearing their own property, while local governments handle debris on public roads and rights-of-way. After a federal disaster declaration, FEMA may reimburse the local government for eligible removal, but the program is administered locally — contact your county emergency management office for the rules that apply to your area.

**Q:** Can I burn hurricane debris to get rid of it?
**A:** Burning is regulated and is often prohibited during or after a disaster because of air quality, fire risk, and utility-line danger. Check with your local fire department or county before burning anything, and follow any burn bans that are in effect.

**Q:** Is flooded drywall and insulation always a total loss?
**A:** Materials soaked with contaminated floodwater generally should be removed and discarded, but the final decision about what to tear out depends on the type of water, how long it stood, and local building requirements. A licensed contractor or your local building department can advise on what must be replaced.

**Q:** Will my insurance pay for debris removal from my yard?
**A:** Some homeowners policies include a limited debris-removal amount, but coverage varies widely by policy and by what caused the damage. Review your declarations page or ask your insurance company directly, because only your policy terms and your insurer can confirm what applies to your situation.

**Q:** How should I protect my children during cleanup?
**A:** Keep children and pets indoors or well away from the work zone, store tools and chemicals out of reach, and make hand-washing a routine. Talk to them about not touching unknown objects, and review the family-safety checklist in {{INTERNAL: /blog/hurricane-safety-seniors-children-disability/}} for age-appropriate guidance.

**Q:** Where do I report a downed power line on my property?
**A:** Stay far away and call your utility company and local emergency services right away; do not try to move it or the object it touches. Your utility can confirm whether the line is live and dispatch a trained crew.

# Hurricane Insurance: Replacement Cost vs Actual Cash Value, and What Your Payout Really Means

## Direct Answer
"Replacement cost" pays what it costs to rebuild or buy something similar new, while "actual cash value" (ACV) pays that same amount minus depreciation for age and wear. After a hurricane, many policies first pay the ACV portion and later release the depreciation "holdback" once you actually repair or replace the item, which is why your first check may look smaller than you expected. Roof payments in particular are often reduced by a separate schedule or by the roof's age. The exact amounts depend entirely on your policy language, so the most reliable next step is to read your declarations page and talk with your insurance company about how your specific coverage applies.

## What "Replacement Cost" Means in a Homeowners Policy
Replacement cost (RC) is the amount needed to repair or rebuild your home or replace your belongings with materials of similar kind and quality, without deducting for depreciation. On paper, it is the more generous basis, because it aims to put you back where you were before the loss. However, replacement-cost payment is usually conditional — insurers generally require that you actually complete the repair or replacement before they release the full amount. Your policy may also cap the dwelling payment at the "dwelling limit," so the RC figure is not unlimited.

## What "Actual Cash Value" Means (and Why It Pays Less)
Actual cash value is replacement cost minus depreciation. A five-year-old roof, a ten-year-old water heater, and worn flooring all lose value over time, and ACV reflects that reduced worth. If your policy covers a category on an ACV basis, you may receive only a fraction of the cost to buy it new. This basis is common for older policies, for certain personal-property items, and for roof payments under a schedule. Understanding which basis applies to each part of your home helps explain why a payout may differ from your repair estimate.

## Why Your First Insurance Check May Be for ACV
Many hurricane claims are paid in stages rather than in one lump sum. The initial payment often reflects actual cash value, with the depreciated portion held back. This structure protects insurers against overpayment when repairs are never made, and it is a normal part of many claims — not a sign that you are being shortchanged. The withheld depreciation is typically recoverable once you submit proof that the work was completed or the item was replaced. Ask your insurer for the timeline and the documents they need to release the holdback.

## Depreciation Holdback and the Second Payment
The "depreciation holdback" is the gap between ACV and full replacement cost. To recover it, you generally must show that you actually repaired or replaced the damaged property, often with receipts and a final invoice. Keep every receipt, because the holdback is released based on documented spending, not on a verbal request. If you do some work yourself, ask your insurer in advance what documentation they accept, since rules vary. The record-keeping approach in {{INTERNAL: /blog/hurricane-documents-insurance-checklist/}} is built for exactly this purpose.

## How Roof Payouts Often Work After a Hurricane
Roofs are treated specially in many hurricane-prone states because they are the most exposed surface. A policy may pay roof losses on a replacement-cost basis, an ACV basis, or a "roof schedule" that pays a percentage of replacement cost depending on the roof's age. An older roof can therefore receive a substantially smaller payment than a newer one, even for the same visible damage. Your declarations page or a separate endorsement usually states the roof basis, so review it before assuming the payout will match a contractor's full estimate.

## The Dwelling Limit vs Personal Property Limit
Your policy separates the structure (dwelling limit) from your belongings (personal property limit), and each may use a different valuation basis. The dwelling limit is the most the policy will pay to rebuild the house itself, while personal property covers furniture, clothing, electronics, and similar items. If you are underinsured — your limit is below the true rebuild cost — no valuation method will make up the gap. Reviewing both limits annually, and after renovations, is one of the most useful habits described in {{INTERNAL: /blog/hurricane-insurance-deductibles-guide/}}.

## Replacement Cost on Personal Belongings
Personal property is sometimes covered at replacement cost and sometimes at actual cash value, depending on the policy and the item. A "replacement cost on contents" endorsement can mean you recover the cost of a new equivalent item rather than the depreciated value of the old one. Even with that endorsement, you typically must replace the item to receive the full amount, and you must document what you owned. The inventory method in {{INTERNAL: /blog/hurricane-contractor-scams-insurance-claim/}} helps establish both value and ownership.

## Named-Storm and Hurricane Deductibles Still Apply
Valuation basis is separate from deductibles, but the two interact. Many coastal policies have a separate hurricane or named-storm deductible calculated as a percentage of the dwelling limit, which is subtracted before any RC or ACV math begins. That means even a full replacement-cost payment is reduced by your deductible first. Read the deductible section of your policy, because the percentage and the trigger event are defined in your specific contract, not by a universal rule.

## How a Proof-of-Loss and Inventory Affect Your Value
A clear proof-of-loss and a room-by-room inventory strengthen your position on valuation. Insurers use your documentation to verify what existed, its condition, and its worth before the storm. Photos taken in calm weather, receipts, and model numbers all support a replacement-cost argument and reduce disputes over depreciation. Submitting a thorough, organized claim also speeds the process, which matters when you are waiting on funds to start repairs. The claim-documentation walkthrough in {{INTERNAL: /blog/hurricane-insurance-claim-guide-documentation/}} expands on this.

## When an Insurance Appraisal or Adjuster Disagrees
If you and your insurer disagree on value, your policy may include an appraisal provision that uses neutral appraisers to settle the amount of loss. This is different from a lawsuit and is often a faster, structured path, but the details — and whether it applies — are set by your policy. A licensed public adjuster can also help document and present your claim, usually for a fee. Before signing anything, confirm the person is licensed in your state and understand the cost, because only your policy and your insurer define what you are owed.

## Steps to Support the Value Your Policy Promises
You can take practical steps that make the valuation work in your favor:
- Photograph and list high-value items before storm season.
- Keep receipts for major purchases in a cloud folder or safe.
- Ask your insurer which categories are RC versus ACV, in writing.
- Save all repair estimates and final invoices.
- Request the depreciation-holdback release procedure up front.
These steps pair naturally with the broader readiness plan in {{INTERNAL: /tools/preparedness-checklist/}}, which keeps your financial documents organized alongside your physical supplies.

## Common Misunderstandings About Payout Amounts
Several myths cause surprise at claim time. A payout is not automatically the full rebuild cost if your limit is too low. A first check is not the final number if depreciation is recoverable. A roof payment is not always the same basis as the rest of the structure. And a neighbor's payment is not a reliable benchmark, because policies differ. The clearest answers come from your own policy and your own insurer, not from a generic rule.

## Reviewing Your Coverage Before Next Season
The best time to understand valuation is before a storm is named, not during a claim. Pull your declarations page, confirm your dwelling and contents limits, note each ACV-versus-RC distinction, and ask your agent about any gap between your limit and current rebuild costs. If you rent or live in a community association, the structure may be covered by a different policy, so coordinate accordingly. For renters, the separate responsibilities are outlined in {{INTERNAL: /blog/apartment-hurricane-preparedness-renters-checklist/}}.

## FAQ

**Q:** Will my insurance pay the full replacement cost automatically after a hurricane?
**A:** Not necessarily. Many policies pay actual cash value first and release depreciation only after you repair or replace the item, and some categories — especially roofs — use a separate, reduced basis. The exact treatment depends on your policy language, so confirm the specifics with your insurance company.

**Q:** What is a depreciation holdback and how do I get it back?
**A:** It is the depreciated portion withheld from the initial payment; you generally recover it by proving you completed the repair or replacement with receipts and a final invoice. Ask your insurer for their required documentation before you start work, because the process varies by company.

**Q:** Why did my roof payment seem much lower than my contractor's estimate?
**A:** Roofs are often paid on a schedule tied to the roof's age, or on an actual-cash-value basis, so older roofs receive less. Your declarations page or a roof endorsement should state the basis, and your insurer can explain how it was calculated for your claim.

**Q:** Does a hurricane deductible reduce my replacement-cost payment?
**A:** Yes, in most cases the hurricane or named-storm deductible is subtracted first, before the RC or ACV calculation is applied to the remaining loss. The percentage and trigger are defined in your policy, so review that section or ask your agent.

**Q:** Should I hire a public adjuster to dispute the value?
**A:** A licensed public adjuster can help document and present your claim, typically for a fee, and your policy may also include an appraisal process for settling amount-of-loss disputes. Verify any adjuster is licensed in your state and understand the cost before signing, and discuss options with your insurer first.

**Q:** How can I check whether my belongings are covered at replacement cost?
**A:** Review your declarations page and any "contents" endorsement, or call your insurance company and ask specifically which personal-property categories use replacement cost versus actual cash value. Keeping a written record of that answer helps avoid surprises at claim time.

# Finding Official Hurricane Information and Avoiding Rumors: A Source-by-Source Guide

## Direct Answer
For the storm itself, start with the National Hurricane Center at https://www.nhc.noaa.gov/, which issues the official forecast, track, and warnings. For what the storm means for your specific county — evacuations, shelters, road closures — rely on your local National Weather Service office, your county or city emergency management (OEM), and your state emergency agency. NOAA Weather Radio provides a non-internet backup when cell and broadband fail. Before sharing anything, confirm it against one of those official sources, because rumors and old screenshots spread quickly during active storms. A live tracking view such as {{INTERNAL: /hurricane-tracker/live/}} is useful for context, but official orders still come from local government.

## Start With the National Hurricane Center for the Storm
The National Hurricane Center (NHC) is the authoritative U.S. source for tropical cyclone location, intensity, and the official forecast cone. Its advisories, discussions, and key messages tell you where the storm is headed and what watches and warnings are in effect. NHC does not order evacuations — that is a local decision — but its data is the foundation for every local alert. Reading the cone correctly matters, and {{INTERNAL: /blog/how-to-read-the-hurricane-cone-of-uncertainty/}} explains the uncertainty band so you do not over-trust a single line.

## Your Local National Weather Service Office
Beyond the NHC's basin-wide products, your local NWS office issues the county-level warnings, flood alerts, and tornado warnings that apply to your community. These offices know local geography — which rivers flood, which bridges close — and they push alerts through NOAA Weather Radio and wireless emergency systems. Bookmark your local office's page from https://www.weather.gov/ and turn on its alert notifications. Local NWS messaging is where generic storm forecasts become specific, actionable guidance for your street.

## County and City Emergency Management (OEM)
Your county or city Office of Emergency Management is the source for evacuation orders, shelter locations, curfews, and sandbag distribution. These decisions are made locally based on NHC and NWS data plus ground conditions, so OEM outranks any screenshot circulating online. Sign up for your county's alert system if one is offered, and follow its official website and verified social accounts. When an order is issued, follow it promptly rather than waiting for secondhand confirmation from friends.

## State Emergency Management Agencies
State emergency management agencies coordinate resources across counties and often run the official public-information line during a disaster. They aggregate road status, shelter capacity, and state-level assistance, and they link to federal help when a major disaster is declared. Your state agency's site is a good middle layer between county orders and federal programs. After a declared disaster, the state also helps connect residents to assistance explained at https://www.disasterassistance.gov/.

## Official Evacuation and Shelter Decisions
Evacuation zones and shelter openings are decided by local government, not by forecasters or by social media. If you are unsure whether you are in a mandatory zone, check your county OEM or the official zone lookup such as {{INTERNAL: /blog/how-to-check-your-hurricane-evacuation-zone/}}. Leave early if ordered — roads clog and conditions worsen as the storm nears. Pet-friendly and special-needs shelter details are also set locally, so confirm those through official channels rather than assuming.

## NOAA Weather Radio as a Non-Internet Backup
Cell networks and broadband often fail during and after a hurricane, which is exactly when you need alerts most. A NOAA Weather Radio with Specific Area Message Encoding (SAME) codes keeps broadcasting official warnings without internet or phone service. Program it for your county, keep spare batteries, and treat it as part of your kit alongside the supply plan in {{INTERNAL: /blog/hurricane-emergency-kit-checklist/}}. It is the most reliable way to hear a tornado warning or evacuation update when everything else goes dark.

## Reverse-911, Wireless Emergency Alerts, and Texts
Wireless Emergency Alerts (WEA) push mandatory messages — including hurricane and evacuation warnings — directly to compatible phones, with no app or signup required. Many counties also use reverse-911 or opt-in text systems for localized updates. Keep your phone's emergency-alert setting enabled and avoid silencing it during storm season. These systems are described among the official alert types in {{INTERNAL: /blog/nws-hurricane-alert-meanings/}}, which helps you act the moment a message arrives.

## Official Mobile Apps vs Social Media Rumors
Official apps from NHC, NWS, FEMA, and your state agency are built to deliver verified information, while social platforms optimize for engagement, not accuracy. A dramatic video or "confirmed" post can be years old, from another storm, or entirely fabricated. Use apps and agency accounts as your primary feed, and treat any unverified post as a rumor until an official source confirms it. The live-tools guide at {{INTERNAL: /blog/how-to-use-hurricane-tracker-live-tools/}} shows how to combine official feeds into one useful view.

## How to Spot and Stop a Hurricane Rumor
Rumors share recognizable traits: absolute claims ("guaranteed"), urgency ("share before it's deleted"), missing source, mismatched dates, and emotional language. Before reposting, ask: who issued this, when, and is it on an official .gov site? If you cannot trace it to NHC, NWS, OEM, or a state agency, do not spread it. When you see a false claim, reply with the official link rather than arguing, and let verified sources do the correcting. Slow, boring, sourced updates are almost always more trustworthy than fast, sensational ones.

## Verifying Images and "Live" Storm Footage
During active storms, the same clip of storm surge or damage circulates labeled as "live" in a dozen cities. Reverse-image search, check the original poster, and look for a date and location in the official caption. Genuine official imagery is usually released by NWS, NHC, NASA, or local government with clear metadata. If a video has no verifiable source and no official echo, treat it as unconfirmed. Remember that your personal safety decisions should rest on warnings from authorities, not on viral footage.

## Why Crowdsourced Maps Need an Official Check
Community-driven maps of floods, outages, and road closures can be helpful for situational awareness, but they are only as accurate as their contributors and can lag reality. Use them to understand trends, never as a substitute for an evacuation order or a closed-road notice. Cross-check any route or hazard against your county OEM and state transportation department before you act on it. The tracker context in {{INTERNAL: /blog/what-hurricane-watch-warning-means/}} helps you separate a watch (be ready) from a warning (act now).

## Building a Trusted-Source List Before the Storm
The time to find official sources is in calm weather, not at the height of a storm when sites are slow. Before season, save these in one note: NHC, your local NWS office, county OEM, state agency, FEMA, and a NOAA radio SAME code list. Add the official accounts you will check and disable non-essential notifications that could bury real alerts. A pre-built source list turns panic-scrolling into a calm, two-minute verification routine. Pair it with the communications plan in {{INTERNAL: /blog/hurricane-family-communication-plan/}} so everyone in the household knows where to look, and keep the physical supply checklist in {{INTERNAL: /tools/preparedness-checklist/}} alongside it.

## What to Do When Sources Disagree
Occasionally a national headline and a local order seem to conflict, or two agencies phrase things differently. When that happens, local government orders (evacuation, shelter, curfew) override general commentary — they reflect on-the-ground conditions. If the conflict is about the storm track, defer to NHC and your local NWS office, and wait for an updated advisory rather than guessing. When in doubt, contact your county emergency management line or local officials for clarification instead of relying on interpretation from unofficial posts.

## FAQ

**Q:** Which source should I trust first for hurricane warnings?
**A:** For the storm forecast and watches/warnings, use the National Hurricane Center at https://www.nhc.noaa.gov/; for what to do in your area — evacuations, shelters, closures — use your county or city emergency management and your local NWS office at https://www.weather.gov/. Local government orders are the final word for action.

**Q:** How do I know if an evacuation order is real?
**A:** Check your county OEM website, its verified social accounts, and any county alert system you signed up for; local government issues these orders, not forecasters or social media. If you are unsure whether you are in a zone, use the official lookup such as {{INTERNAL: /blog/how-to-check-your-hurricane-evacuation-zone/}}.

**Q:** My cell service is down — how do I still get alerts?
**A:** A NOAA Weather Radio with SAME codes broadcasts official warnings without internet or phone service, and Wireless Emergency Alerts can still reach compatible phones where signal exists. Keep spare batteries and program the radio for your county before the season starts.

**Q:** How can I tell if a hurricane video online is fake or old?
**A:** Look for a verifiable source, a date, and a location in the original caption, and cross-check it against NHC, NWS, or local government posts. If the clip has no official echo and no metadata, treat it as unconfirmed and do not base safety decisions on it.

**Q:** Are social media posts from "breaking" accounts reliable for hurricanes?

**Q:** Who do I contact if I see a false hurricane rumor causing panic?
